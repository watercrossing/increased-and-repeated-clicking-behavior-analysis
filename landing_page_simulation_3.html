The four highlighted cues

  1. Suspicious sender address – Check if the email address matches the official domain of the sender. For instance, an email from a colleague at [anonymized institution] should originate from an @[anonymized domain] address.
  2. No personal greeting – Generic salutations instead of your real name can indicate phishing.
  3. Threatening with negative consequences – Threatening with negative consequences if not acting urgently - Online scams often try to make you feel like you need to do something right away. Whenever this happens, stop and first verify with someone else you know whether this is a legitimate, urgent action you need to take.
  4. Misleading hyperlinks – Do not click on links blindly. A link labeled "Click here" could lead to a fraudulent website. Always double-check the destination by hovering over the link and checking the URL in the bottom left corner of your browser.

  • Persuasion tactics - Scammers often pose as authorities or a reputable source to request something from you.
    Example: A fake email from your "IT department" asks you to provide login details to fix an urgent security issue.
  • Extreme consequences - Be wary of emails that promise extravagant rewards, severe penalties or unsolicited requests.
    Example: "You have won a free vacation! Click here to claim your prize!" or "Your account will be permanently disabled unless you act now!"
What to Do Next Time

Whenever an email asks you to take action (e.g., downloading an attachment, clicking on a link, updating information, transferring money, etc.), follow these steps:

  • Check the URL - Compare the link in the phishing email to the official website's URL.
    Example: If an email claims to be from PayPal but the link directs to "paypaal.com" instead of "paypal.com", it is a phishing attempt. Always verify links before clicking.
  • Look for hidden hyperlinks - Ensure displayed URLs match the real ones.
    Example: A hyperlink may say "www.yourbank.com," but when hovered over, it actually links to "www.your-bank.com".
  • Check the return-path - The metadata often reveals inconsistencies.
    Steps:
    1. Click on the three dots in the upper right corner of the email.
    2. Select "View" → "View message details".
    3. Look for the "Return-Path" field, which shows the actual email address where a reply would be sent.
    Example: An email claiming to be from "support@amazon.com" but with a return-path of "randomuser@gmail.com" is suspicious.
If You're Still Unsure
